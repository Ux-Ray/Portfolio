Map

Understanding the product, users, and challenge.

We started by reviewing MeteoCast, a professional WebGIS platform for visualizing historical, real-time, and forecast meteorological data. The challenge focused on improving menu organization, data visualization flow, and time navigation.

In this phase, we created How Might We questions and used them to frame possible design opportunities around clarity, speed, and lower cognitive load.

Test

Learning from early user interviews.

The first tests helped us understand how professional users worked with the existing platform. Many users needed fast answers under time pressure, but the navigation, terminology, and temporal structure made this difficult. Based on these findings, and considering the limited time available during the design sprint, we prioritized the HMWs that could have the strongest impact on professional users’ urgent workflows.

1
Navigation was too complex for urgent workflows. Users needed fewer steps to reach the data they used most often.
2
The timeline created confusion. Users expected past, present, and forecast data to feel connected.
3
Map interpretation needed support. Legends, colors, and symbols were not always clear enough for quick decisions.
Sketch

Exploring possible interface directions.

After identifying the main friction points, we sketched different ways to simplify navigation, improve the map workspace, make alerts more visible, and support a clearer relationship between real-time, forecast, and historical data. Before sketching solutions, we also looked at comparable weather and GIS platforms to understand common interaction patterns for dense map-based data, timelines, overlays, alerts, and professional weather monitoring. This competitive analysis helped us collect references and identify ideas that could be adapted to MeteoCast.

Decide

Choosing a more task-oriented direction.

We decided to move away from a purely service-based structure and explore phenomenon-based navigation. This meant organizing the interface around weather situations such as rain, wind, storms, temperature, and visibility.

We also prioritized presets, a unified timebar, clearer search, and a more actionable alert system.

Prototype

Building an interactive concept.

The prototype gave more space to the map, reduced the visual weight of the menu, and introduced clearer access to weather categories, location search, alerts, legends, presets, and time controls.

☔

Phenomenon-based menu

Parameters were grouped by weather phenomena to reduce reliance on technical service names.

＋

Custom presets

Users could save recurring parameter groups for faster access to repeated workflows.

⏱

Unified timebar

The timebar supported movement across past, real-time, and forecast data.

⚠

Actionable alerts

Alerts were designed to communicate risk type, severity, timing, and location.

Test again

Validating and refining the redesign.

The second round of testing showed that the redesigned direction was promising, especially the weather-phenomenon menu and the timebar interaction. However, some areas still needed refinement.

Clearer Users understood several parts of the phenomenon-based navigation.
Needed More visual clarity between past, real-time, and forecast data.
Next Improve presets, onboarding, alert source, probability, and location.
Reflection

Expert tools need managed complexity, not oversimplification.

This project helped me understand that UX for professional platforms is not only about making an interface visually cleaner. It is about helping users make sense of complex information quickly, especially when decisions are time-sensitive.

The main lesson was that the information architecture should reflect how users think and work. In MeteoCast, this meant shifting from service-centered navigation toward workflows based on phenomena, risks, locations, and time.
